# SDM Interview - Case 3: Program Management Analysis

## Question
Tell me about the most complex, cross-team program you've managed. How did you structure it, track progress, and handle dependencies across teams? What was your escalation path when dependencies slipped? How did you keep alignment across teams with different priorities?

---

## Narrative

The candidate described a program called Dynamic Blocks, where the goal was to bring personalized content into marketing emails the same way it already worked on the website. The marketing team was producing 10-20K pieces of content (football, baseball, and so on), and the idea was to build reusable blocks that could be plugged into emails so they didn't have to create content from scratch for every campaign. The candidate said they drove this end to end and were the single point for setting up the prioritization and the overall program. They started by drawing up a module diagram showing how a dynamic block would look and what was needed to make it work, mainly the CMS team building sub-templates and the rules team putting a rules engine in place. They took this to their principal engineer during the ideation phase, who then turned it into a high-level and system design. From there, the candidate worked with engineering managers across the different teams, walked them through the design, agreed on who owned what, collected LOEs from each team, and built a dependency chart showing which pieces (like the rules engine) had to come before others. They then presented the tech roadmap to the product manager and senior leadership. For execution, the candidate ran the sprint-level planning across teams, set up a Confluence page to track module deliverables, and held a monthly meeting to review progress, with closer reviews one sprint ahead of any upcoming delivery. When timelines slipped, the escalation path was to give the product owner an early heads-up on the launch date moving. The candidate also built a dependency tree to figure out the real impact of any slip, and looked for items that could be pulled forward and done in parallel to recover time. The program took eight months to launch and delivered click/opt-in rate going from 1.1% to roughly 2%, email conversion from 0.2% to 0.7% (about $45M per quarter), and content creation dropping from 1,000 to 600 pieces because the same content could be reused across modular blocks.

The candidate clearly knows how to run a cross-team program. Acting as the orchestrator across CMS, rules, and other teams, building a module diagram and dependency chart, collecting LOEs, running sprint-level planning, and tying it all to a tracker with a monthly cadence are the right mechanics for a program of this size. The business outcomes are strong and concrete (click-through nearly doubled, email conversion 3.5x, $45M/quarter, 40% reduction in content creation effort), and an eight-month delivery across multiple teams is a credible result. That said, a few things fall short of the SDM bar. The escalation path was thin, just "give the product owner a heads-up", with no clear story on when to escalate, to whom beyond that, or how trade-offs were resolved when teams had competing priorities. The question specifically asked how the candidate kept alignment across teams with different priorities, and that part wasn't really answered; the story focused on planning and tracking, not on the harder work of pulling teams in the same direction when their own roadmaps were pulling them elsewhere. The cadence also sounded light for a program of this scope, "an offline sync and meet once a month" leaves a lot of room for things to drift between check-ins, and the candidate didn't talk about risk management, change control, or how they handled scope changes during the eight months. The personal contribution is also a bit blurred in places: the candidate started the design work, but the principal engineer turned it into the HLD and system design, and it wasn't fully clear what the candidate owned versus supported through the rest of the program. Overall, the candidate shows solid program management instincts and delivered a real business outcome, but the depth on cross-team alignment, escalation, and risk handling isn't quite at the level expected for SDM-scale program ownership.
